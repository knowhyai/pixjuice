Game Feel Lab

Game feel is how your game behaves in the player's hands. The lab splits it into four domains — support (response & forgiveness), physicality, amplification and context — and ships one live recipe at a time: a demo you can toggle, retune and compare, with engine code that follows the sliders.

Sources & method

Numbers on this site carry one of two labels: a published source, or 经验值 (heuristic) — the industry's oral tradition. Heuristics are fine slider defaults; they are not findings. Retune everything.

  • Steve Swink, Game Feel (2008) — the lab's working definition and its six dimensions. Precursor article ↗
  • Pichlmair & Johansen, “Designing Game Feel: A Survey”, IEEE Transactions on Games (2022; arXiv 2020) — the three design domains this section is built on. arXiv ↗
  • Jonasson & Purho, “Juice It or Lose It” (2012) — the toggle-and-compare demo method every recipe here copies. Demo source ↗
  • Nijman, “The Art of Screenshake” (2013) — screenshake, knockback and impact layered one decision at a time.
  • Song, “Improving the Combat ‘Impact’ of Action Games” (2005) — animation, frame freezing, camera, VFX, rumble and layered audio. Article ↗
  • Masahiro Sakurai, Famitsu column vol. 490–491 — hitstop design in fighting games. Translation ↗
  • SmashWiki, “Hitlag” — per-game hitlag formulas and caps. Wiki ↗
  • Lin et al., “What Features Influence Impact Feel?” (2022) — ranks impact feel across 44 action games; hit stop, sound consistency and camera control as the critical three. arXiv ↗
  • Kao, “The Effects of Juiciness in an Action RPG” (2020) — moderate juiciness beats both none and extreme. DOI ↗
  • Thorson, “Celeste's forgiveness” (2020) and Fasterholdt et al., “You Say Jump, I Say How High?” (2016) — measured windows and jump parameters for the upcoming Support and Physicality recipes. Post ↗
